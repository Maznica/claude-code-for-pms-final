The plain version

It was the timer. Not the weighting everybody blamed.

4.2 shipped two routing changes on the same day. The team's read — and my predecessor's — was that re-weighting proximity against acceptance history had pushed responders down the list. It hadn't. The ping wait went from 90 seconds to 60, people started missing calls they used to catch, and the code scores a miss exactly like a refusal.

The trap is the last arrow. Points are only earned by answering a ping, so a responder who has stopped receiving pings has no mechanism to recover. Nothing in 232 lines of routing code ever raises a score on its own. The weight change (proximity 0.45→0.60, acceptance 0.40→0.25) arithmetically softens that spiral — it is the innocent half of the release.

The decisive query

Measure the gap between pings and the timer confesses.

The pings table has no delivery field and no time-to-accept. But when a callout moves on to the next responder, the gap between consecutive pings is the wait. If somebody tapped no, the gap is unchanged. If nobody answered, it moves by exactly what the config moved.

Seconds between one ping and the next

A thirty-second cut in the config, showing up as a thirty-second cut in the data — and only on the branch where the timer runs.

MODULE 01

Origin Story — the consensus was wrong

The finding

The cause is the timeout cut, not the weight change everyone blamed. config.py:9 went 90s to 60s. Misses jumped 2.3% to 18.0% on 12 August; refusals fell from 21.1% to 18.0%.

The amplifier: offer.py:30 and history.py:35 penalise a miss identically to a refusal (−0.12 against +0.08 credit), and the score never decays — a TODO dated 2019. Four responders floored out. Written up in overview_questions.md.

Takeaway

Ask what is missing and contradictory across sources, not what the story is. The room had a theory, my predecessor had a theory, and both named the wrong half of the release. Asking the question in a form the documents could fail to answer is what broke it open.

The prompt I wrote
I will ask now three questions. Please answer them separately as detailed as possible: 1. What's contradictory or missing, not just in the company documents, but across Rook's wiki, its database and the files in my repo? 2. How exactly does the dispatch algorithm determine which responder gets contacted first, and how did that logic change in Release 4.2? 3. Is the decline in response rate caused by responders ignoring notifications, or by notifications failing to reach them?
Question 3 is the one that mattered. It forces a choice between two mechanisms instead of inviting a narrative, and the gap-between-pings query is what settled it.
MODULE 02

Super Hearing — volume is not prevalence

The finding

Our support lead read the tickets as two thirds "my phone never goes off" and one third "it buzzes and it's gone". By volume she was right. By how many people are affected it inverts:

Also unreported anywhere: out-of-area pings went 17% to 39% — responders travelling further, invisible in acceptance rate. And tickets and interviews name completely different suppressed responders, with no overlap. Neither source alone gives the right four.

Takeaway

Disagreeing with the model is the work. I pushed back on its grouping twice, and the structure that survived — three defects, ranked by urgency — is the one that went into the brief. A tidy synthesis I had not argued with would have kept the support lead's split intact and buried the bigger problem.

MODULE 03

Rewind — refusals cannot sink anyone

The finding

This answers the question our engineering manager asked on 14 August and nobody had answered since: was the change meant to apply to responders who turn jobs down?

With +0.08 credit against −0.12 penalty, the break-even taken rate is exactly 60%. To cross it on refusals alone you would have to refuse 40% of your pings. The roster record is Meteor Mite's 28.6%. Refusals cannot sink anybody. Misses crossed it for four people.

And a correction to my own earlier reading: Group B does not exist. Two responders I had counted as suppressed are, demand-adjusted, +14% and +16% — their areas simply had the two biggest incident drops. The damage is contained to four, not six.

Takeaway

Normalise before you accuse. Raw ping counts said six people were being starved; incidents-in-their-own-area said four. Eastgate is the control that proves it — Meteor Mite −59% and The Gale +74%, same area, same handler.

The prompt I wrote
Use 3 examples and visualize it to back up following hypothesis: "The change applies to everyone's existing score. But before 4.2, people's turn-downs had been wiped out by the 1.0 ceiling, so old history made almost no difference. After 4.2, turning jobs down didn't sink anyone either. What decided it was missed pings, because the code counts a miss as a turn-down. So the change didn't hit people who turn jobs down, old or new. It hit people who didn't answer within 60 seconds."

Why old history didn't matter

All 16 responders had hit the 1.00 ceiling before 4.2, discarding 315 acceptance credits. Old refusal history wasn't outweighed — it was erased. The responder who refused most on the roster sat at a perfect 1.00 on 11 August.

MODULE 04

X-Ray Vision — the floor is a basin

The finding

The whole routing service is 232 lines and contains four if statements, none of which looks at who the responder is. Nothing was grandfathered; there is nowhere for a per-responder distinction to live. Exactly one line adds points — history.py:27, gated on receiving a ping.

And the thing that changes the plan: the clamp makes the break-even yes-rate vary by height. 0% at the floor, 50% at 0.08, 60% at 0.12 and above. The four sit at roughly 35–45%, so they bounce between 0.00 and 0.08 and cannot climb out. Restoring the timer will not recover them. It stops new people falling in while the headline metric recovers around them.

Takeaway

When the code and the data disagree, that gap is the finding. An empty function body means the answer is elsewhere, and a parameter name is not a behaviour. offer.py:25 says it walks the whole ranked list — but 82 of 97 failed callouts died after a single ping. "Nobody came" turned out to mean the one person we asked said no.

The prompt I wrote
Before we add this, let's elaborate on this even more? Can you find something in the data or code why the ranking list is so short in nobody came cases. The only thing I can think off is that someone sets me as not available. I disagree with you that you are excluded from the list when your expected travel time is larger than 45 minutes. From all the discussions, I understood that if this is the case your proximity score is zero but you still are part of the list. What I miss here?
That disagreement was right and produced a correction: there is no region gate, and the 45-minute horizon removes nobody — it sets proximity to 0.0 and they stay on the list. Availability is the only filter.

Two instrumentation gaps

No travel-time estimate is stored — the largest input, weighted 0.60, leaves zero trace. And no availability history, the only thing that can shorten the candidate list. Both make whole classes of question unanswerable.

MODULE 05

Super Speed — the fix, and a prototype of it

The artifact

A brief for the director (05-super-speed/brief.md) and a clickable prototype (05-super-speed/prototype.html) showing the handler console and the responder's phone, before and after, with a live timer and a test ping.

The rescue value is 0.8, not NEUTRAL_SCORE 0.5 — 0.5 would leave the four below everyone else. 0.8 is both the counterfactual resting place and mid-roster today. Plus a floor of about three offers a week, as a backstop and a detector, against a baseline of roughly ten offers per responder per week.

Takeaway

Build the thing that shows the trade-off, not the thing that wins the argument. The prototype puts both numbers on screen at once on purpose: restoring 90 seconds takes acceptance back to about 76% while the four stay at zero. That is why the timer decision comes last in the plan, not first.

MODULE 06

Sidekicks — so it doesn't happen again

4.2 was not a coding mistake. It was a decision that shipped without anyone writing down how we would know it had worked. review-checklist is a project skill that grades every brief against five criteria before it becomes a release.

The five criteria
Owner nameda person or team, not a role with nobody in it
Success measurehow anyone would know it worked, checkably
Scope stays boundedthe ask at the end is the ask at the start
Problem before fixopens with who hurts, not what to build
Evidence present and currentfigures a reader can re-run, with a date
It reads and reports. It never edits a brief and never files anything. Defined in .claude/skills/review-checklist/SKILL.md, scheduled Monday 09:06; runs land in 06-sidekicks/review-checklist-runs/.
First real run: four of Priya's wiki briefs

Every one of them fails on evidence. Not a single figure between them. Each case rests on one anecdote: "Halloran's asked more than once", "Aunt Dot asked for".

The prompt I wrote
Imagine it's Monday morning review-checklist flagged four gaps overnight, on four briefs in Rook's wiki that nobody in this room has read yet. You don't know yet what they mean. But you already know exactly where to look, without doing any of the digging yourself. What I should write as a prompt to test the skill in this scenario? What should I write as a prompt to test it on the brief created in module 5
Takeaway

Automation earns trust by being checked, not by running. The course-supplied sample output claimed one flag per brief; the real run found nine, and the sample was dated eleven days before this repository's first commit — for a skill and a folder that did not exist. The skill now tells itself to ignore it. It also graded my own Module 5 brief: 1 flag of 5, no owner named on the Supply step.

Where it stands

One small change, and three things we should have been recording.

The ask

Stop counting silence as a refusal. One line. Everything else in the brief follows from it — and without it, restoring the timer makes the weekly number look healthy while four people stay at zero and Eastgate keeps dropping incidents.

The honest caveat

The ping data stops 6 September and it is now November. Everything here is two months stale. First action is to ask our analyst for current numbers before anyone acts on it — which is exactly the criterion my own skill would flag me on.

No delivery field
We cannot tell a phone that never rang from a phone nobody answered
No time-to-accept
A headline metric we report weekly and cannot reconstruct
No channel to responders
All 147 tickets came from handlers. The two handlers of the worst-hit filed none

The responders who were hurt most are the ones we never heard from. That is the part a dashboard was never going to tell us.
