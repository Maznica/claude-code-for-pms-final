The plain version

It was the timer, not the weighting everybody blamed.

4.2 shipped two routing changes on the same day. The team's read, and my predecessor's, was that re-weighting proximity against acceptance history had pushed responders down the list. It hadn't. The ping wait went from 90 seconds to 60, people started missing calls they used to catch, and the code scores a miss exactly like a refusal.

The decisive query

Measure the gap between pings and the timer gives itself away.

The pings table has no delivery field and no time-to-accept. But when a callout moves on to the next responder, the gap between consecutive pings is the wait. If somebody tapped no, the gap is unchanged. If nobody answered, it moves by exactly what the config moved.

01Origin Story

The consensus was wrong.

The finding

The cause is the timeout cut, not the weight change everyone blamed. config.py:9 went 90s to 60s. The amplifier: a miss is penalised identically to a refusal, and the score never decays (a TODO dated 2019). The threshold was exact: four or more misses in the first five days and you were gone.

Testing the “seasonal” theory
Priya was partly right: incidents really did dip. But acceptance is a ratio. Fewer callouts don't make people answer a smaller share of them.
Takeaway

Ask what is missing and contradictory across sources, not what the story is. The room had a theory, my predecessor had a theory, and both named the wrong half of the release.

The prompt I wrote
I will ask now three questions. Please answer them separately as detailed as possible: 1. What's contradictory or missing, not just in the company documents, but across Rook's wiki, its database and the files in my repo? 2. How exactly does the dispatch algorithm determine which responder gets contacted first, and how did that logic change in Release 4.2? 3. Is the decline in response rate caused by responders ignoring notifications, or by notifications failing to reach them?
Question 3 is the one that mattered. It forces a choice between two mechanisms instead of inviting a narrative.

03Rewind

Refusals cannot sink anyone.

This answers the question our engineering manager asked on 14 August: was the change meant to apply to responders who turn jobs down? With +0.08 against −0.12, the break-even is a 60% taken rate. To sink on refusals alone you'd need to refuse 40% of your pings.

Refusal rate needed vs record
Nobody came close. Misses crossed the line for four people.
Demand-adjusted change in pings
Normalised to incidents in their own area. Group B does not exist: Ashgrove and Halfmoon were up, not suppressed. Same area, same handler: Meteor Mite −59, The Gale +74.
The tickets told it in order
Cause, then effect, five days apart. Tickets peak two weeks after misses, so the metric recovers while complaints are still rising.
Takeaway

Normalise before you accuse. Raw ping counts said six people were being starved; incidents in their own area said four. And old refusal history didn't matter: all 16 had hit the 1.00 ceiling before 4.2, throwing away 315 credits.

The prompt I wrote
Use 3 examples and visualize it to back up following hypothesis: "The change applies to everyone's existing score. But before 4.2, people's turn-downs had been wiped out by the 1.0 ceiling, so old history made almost no difference. After 4.2, turning jobs down didn't sink anyone either. What decided it was missed pings, because the code counts a miss as a turn-down. So the change didn't hit people who turn jobs down, old or new. It hit people who didn't answer within 60 seconds."
04X-Ray Vision

The floor is a basin, not a pit.

232 lines, four if statements, none of which looks at who the responder is. Exactly one line adds points (history.py:27), and only when a ping arrives. Then the clamp turns the floor into a trap.

Above 0.00 the bar rises faster than they can answer, so they bounce between 0.00 and 0.08. Restoring the timer will not recover them. It stops new people falling in while the headline recovers around them.

Takeaway

When the code and the data disagree, that gap is the finding. offer.py:25 says it walks the whole list, yet 82 of 97 failures stopped at one ping. “Nobody came” meant the one person we asked said no.

The prompt I wrote
Before we add this, let's elaborate on this even more? Can you find something in the data or code why the ranking list is so short in nobody came cases. The only thing I can think off is that someone sets me as not available. I disagree with you that you are excluded from the list when your expected travel time is larger than 45 minutes. From all the discussions, I understood that if this is the case your proximity score is zero but you still are part of the list. What I miss here?
That disagreement was right: there is no region gate. The 45-minute horizon sets proximity to 0.0 and they stay on the list. Availability is the only filter.

06Sidekicks

So it doesn't happen again.

4.2 was not a coding mistake. It was a decision that shipped without anyone writing down how we would know it had worked. review-checklist grades every brief against five criteria before it becomes a release.

First real runs: four wiki briefs + my own
passesflagged
Every one of Priya's briefs fails on evidence. Not a single figure between them. Each case rests on one anecdote: “Halloran's asked more than once”, “Aunt Dot asked for”. My own Module 5 brief: 1 flag, no owner on the Supply step.
Takeaway

Automation earns trust by being checked, not by running. The course-supplied sample output claimed one flag per brief; the real run found nine, and the sample was dated eleven days before this repository's first commit.

Where it stands

One small change, and the things we should have been recording.

The ask

Stop counting silence as a refusal. One line. Everything else in the brief follows from it. Without it, restoring the timer makes the weekly number look healthy while four people stay at zero and Eastgate keeps dropping incidents.

The honest caveat

The ping data stops 6 September and it is now November. Everything here is two months stale. First action: ask our analyst for current numbers before anyone acts on it.

The whole instrument: 19 columns across 5 tables, and what's missing
pingscalloutsrespondershandlerssupport_tickets ✗ delivered?✗ time-to-accept✗ travel-time estimate (weight 0.60) ✗ availability history✗ score✗ a channel to responders
We can't tell a phone that never rang from a phone nobody answered. All 147 tickets came from handlers, and the two handlers of the worst-hit filed none.

The responders who were hurt most are the ones we never heard from. That is the part a dashboard was never going to tell us.
