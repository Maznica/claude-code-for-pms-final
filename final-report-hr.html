Two weeks into a PM role I inherited a release that had landed badly, a team with a confident theory about why, and a predecessor's note telling me not to pull it apart. The theory was wrong. Finding that took four independent sources, two corrections to my own conclusions, and a one-line fix that the obvious fix would have hidden.

What this artifact is

A worked product investigation, start to finish: ambiguous signal → diagnosis → quantified trade-off → prototype → a reusable process change so it is caught earlier next time. Built over six working sessions with Claude Code as the instrument.

01 · The situation

What I walked into

Rook Dispatch routes emergency callouts to a roster of independent responders. A console offers a callout to the best-ranked available responder; it is taken, turned down, or missed; if not taken it moves to the next person.

Release 4.2 shipped eleven days before I joined. Since then the headline metric — callout acceptance rate — had fallen, support tickets were running at roughly three times normal, and a handler had emailed our support lead directly, which she said never happens.

Three things made this harder than a normal regression:

  • Two changes shipped the same day, so the obvious suspect and the real cause were entangled.
  • The organisation had already converged on an answer — a re-weighting of the ranking formula — and my predecessor had explicitly ruled out revisiting it.
  • The people worst affected had no way to tell us. All 147 tickets came from handlers. Responders have no channel at all.

The prevailing theory

"August is always soft seasonally, two things moved in the same release, my read is it's mostly seasonal and will recover in September — look there before pulling the routing change apart."

— my predecessor's handover note, echoed by the engineering manager two weeks later

What was true in it

Incidents genuinely dipped, from about 138 a week in July to 110–127 in mid-August. I checked it rather than dismissing it. But acceptance rate is a ratio — fewer incidents move the numerator and denominator together, so seasonality cannot explain a drop in the rate. Partly right, and not the cause.

02 · The finding

It was the other change in the release.

The ping wait went from 90 seconds to 60. People started missing calls they used to catch. And the scoring code penalises a miss exactly as it penalises a refusal — so missing calls quietly removed people from the rotation, permanently.

The query that settled it

The data had no delivery flag and no time-to-accept, so "did the phone ring?" was not directly answerable. It was answerable indirectly: the gap between one offer and the next is the wait. If someone tapped no, the gap should be unchanged. If nobody answered, it should move by exactly what the config moved.

A thirty-second change in the config appearing as a thirty-second change in the data, on the timer branch only. That is a mechanism, not a correlation.

Why the consensus theory could not be right

takenturned downmissed

Refusals went down, not up. The entire drop is misses. The story everyone was telling — that re-weighting had pushed people down the list and they were disengaging — requires refusals to rise. They fell. One chart retires a theory three people had signed off on.

03 · Method

Four sources that could each have been wrong alone.

No single source produced the answer, and two of them actively misled. The discipline that mattered was refusing to stop at the first source that agreed with me.

04 · Capability

What this demonstrates, with the evidence attached.

Each claim below is backed by something in the repository, not by a self-assessment.

Quantitative analysis

Derived a metric the system never stored

There is no score column anywhere in the database — the ranking score exists only in code. I rebuilt it for all sixteen responders with a recursive SQL replay over the offer history, then ran a counterfactual replay with one rule changed to size the fix before proposing it.

Result: under the current rule, four people hit the floor. Under the proposed rule, zero of sixteen do, and the four sit mid-roster at 0.76–0.84.

Technical fluency

Read the production code and found what the data could not say

The routing service is 232 lines with four conditionals, none of which looks at who the responder is — which definitively answered an open engineering question about whether the change had been targeted. Exactly one line in the system adds points, and it only runs when an offer arrives.

Method that generalises: an empty function body means the answer is elsewhere; a parameter name is not a behaviour; when code and data disagree, that gap is the finding.

Research synthesis

Separated volume from prevalence

Our support lead read the tickets as a 2:1 split between two complaint themes. By ticket count she was right. By how many people were affected it inverts: the minority theme hit 11 of 12 handlers, the majority theme only 4.

Consequence: the loudest problem and the widest problem were not the same problem, and prioritising by volume would have fixed the wrong one first.

Prototyping

Built the artifact instead of describing it

A clickable prototype of the handler console and the responder's phone — before, after, and under three candidate fixes — with a live countdown and a test offer, so a stakeholder can feel the difference between 60 and 90 seconds rather than read about it.

Design choice: it shows the recovering headline metric and the four people still at zero on the same screen, deliberately. The prototype's job was to make the trade-off undeniable, not to win.

Stakeholder judgement

Sequenced a fix against organisational pressure

The politically easy move was to restore the timer: it returns acceptance to roughly 76% within a week and the weekly report looks solved. I put it last in the sequence and said why in writing, because shipping it first makes the metric recover around four people who stay at zero.

Also handled: a predecessor's documented "do not reopen this" position, which I neither ignored nor deferred to — I tested the part of it that was testable and reported which part held.

Process & automation

Turned the lesson into a control

4.2 was not a coding mistake. It was a change that shipped without anyone writing down how we would know it had worked. I built a review skill that grades every brief against five criteria — owner, success measure, bounded scope, problem-before-solution, current evidence — and scheduled it weekly.

First real run: four existing briefs, nine flags. Every one failed on evidence — not a single figure between them. It also graded my own brief, and flagged it.

05 · Judgement

Four times I overturned my own conclusion.

This is the section I would most want a hiring manager to read. Being right at the end mattered less than the willingness to keep testing a conclusion that already felt finished.

What I believedWhat actually heldHow the correction was forced
Six responders were being starved of work. Four. Two of the six were, demand-adjusted, +14% and +16% — their areas simply had the two largest incident drops. Normalising against incidents in each responder's own area instead of using raw offer counts.
Responders beyond a 45-minute horizon are filtered out of the candidate list. Nobody is filtered on distance. The horizon sets a proximity score to zero; they stay on the list. There is no region gate either. I disagreed with an analysis that had sounded plausible, and said so explicitly. Re-reading the code settled it against the earlier reading.
Restoring the timer recovers the affected responders. It does not. The score clamp makes the break-even rate vary by height, so the four bounce near the floor and cannot climb out. The timer fixes the metric, not the people. Working out the arithmetic of the clamp rather than trusting the intuition that undoing a change undoes its effects.
Short candidate lists mean the ranking is excluding people. The code does walk the whole ranked list. A one-name list means only one person was available — which makes it an availability problem, and a different fix. Testing the hypothesis against the ordering code instead of accepting the interpretation that matched the story I already had.

A hypothesis I killed

I suspected a feedback loop into the gear-provisioning product, because Dispatch writes the availability record that it reads. I tested it. Not supported. It is in the write-up as a closed question rather than quietly dropped.

A caveat I put in my own brief

The offer data ends 6 September and it is now November. Everything here is two months stale, and the first action in my plan is to get current numbers before anyone acts on it. My own review skill flags briefs for exactly this, and it would flag mine.

06 · Working with AI

The prompts did the work, so here are three of mine.

I used Claude Code as an analytical instrument across a wiki, a live database and a codebase. The skill being demonstrated is not "can operate an AI tool" — it is knowing what question to put to it, and not accepting the first fluent answer.

Force a mechanism, not a narrative
Is the decline in response rate caused by responders ignoring notifications, or by notifications failing to reach them?

Why it worked: it offers two mutually exclusive mechanisms instead of inviting a story. A question shaped like this can be answered wrong, which is what makes it worth asking. It is what sent me to the gap between offers.

Disagree on the record
I disagree with you that you are excluded from the list when your expected travel time is larger than 45 minutes. From all the discussions, I understood that if this is the case your proximity score is zero but you still are part of the list. What I miss here?

Why it worked: I was right, and the correction went into the permanent record. Treating the model as a colleague who can be wrong is the difference between research and transcription.

Overrule the recommendation
We very likely know the answers from previous modules and therefore it is not necessary to ask wen li. Moreover, maybe Don't count miss as a refusal is most important thing. Then somehow un-stick the four [...] and lastly the decision about the timer. What do you think?

Why it worked: the proposed plan opened with a dependency on a colleague we no longer needed. Re-sequencing it was a product decision, made by me, and the brief was rewritten to match.

What I would tell another PM about working this way

Ask for the gap

"What is contradictory or missing across these sources?" surfaces more than "summarise this". The useful finding is usually the disagreement between two sources, not the content of either.

Verify the automation

Course-supplied sample output for my own skill claimed four flags; the real run found nine, and the sample was dated before the folder it described existed. I now have the skill told to ignore it.

Keep the record

Every session ends by writing findings, corrections and open questions to a context file. Session six can still cite session one — and correct it.

07 · The recommendation

One line of code, and the hard part is the order.

Every responder's standing, replayed

the four affected the other twelve (position inside the stated band is illustrative)

A cliff, not a distribution. Twelve responders sit between 0.72 and 1.00; four sit at exactly zero. Nobody is in between, and nothing in the system will ever move them.

The sequence I proposed

1Stop scoring a miss as a refusal. One line. Everything else follows from it.
2Un-stick the four at 0.8, with a floor of ~3 offers a week as a backstop and a detector.
3Record delivery and time-to-accept — a headline metric we report weekly and cannot reconstruct.
4Check the downstream product, which reads the availability record we write.
5Tell the handlers. They filed 147 tickets and got no answer.
6Then decide on the timer. Last, on purpose.

Why 0.8 and not the system default

The code has a neutral value of 0.5 for unrecognised names, and it is the obvious thing to reach for. It is also wrong here: 0.5 would place the four below every other responder on the roster. 0.8 is both the counterfactual resting place and mid-roster today.

The number came from the replay. That is the difference between a recommendation and a guess.

The second-order harm nobody was measuring

Work did not disappear; it moved. One area concentrated onto a single responder — callouts offered to him and nobody else went from 39% to 70%, and four incidents went unattended in a month. Two other areas exported their work: offers travelling out of area rose from 17% to 39%. None of this appears in acceptance rate, because an offer taken forty minutes away counts exactly like one next door.

Why our only feedback channel reached the people least affected, and what I would build to hear from the people who were.

Instrumentation

Three gaps that make whole classes of question unanswerable — including the largest input to the ranking, which leaves no trace at all.

Process

The five-criterion brief review, why evidence is the one criterion a well-written document cannot fake, and what it found on my own work.

What I would do differently

Faster to the code

I spent two sessions on qualitative and quantitative work before reading the routing service. The code was 232 lines and would have cut the ambiguity earlier.

Earlier on the second-order harm

The concentration and export effects surfaced in session five. They are arguably more serious than the metric drop and should have been looked for sooner.

A channel, not a report

I produced a brief for the people who already had the dashboard. The responders affected still have no way to tell anyone what happened to them.
