The 90-second version

Wrong theory, hidden harm, systemic fix.

The situation

I inherited a product whose last release had landed badly. Acceptance was down, tickets ran ~3× normal, and leadership had already settled on a cause. My predecessor's handover said, in writing, don't reopen it.

What I did

I tested the consensus instead of inheriting it. I rebuilt a metric the system never stored, read the production code, and ran a counterfactual to size the fix before proposing it. I also changed my own mind four times, on the record.

The outcome

The real cause was a one-line rule in the other half of the release. The obvious fix would have restored the headline metric and stranded four people permanently. I sequenced the fix to avoid that, and built a weekly review control so the next release gets checked before it ships.

Five convictions, each with proof

What I believe about leading product, and where this case proved it.

01 · DECISION QUALITY

Consensus is a hypothesis, not a finding.

Three senior people had signed off on one explanation: a ranking change pushed people down the list, they disengaged, and the seasonal dip would recover. That story needs refusals to rise. They fell.

Proof: misses went 2.3% → 18.0% on release day while refusals dropped 21.1% → 18.0%. One chart retired a theory the organisation had already agreed on.

At leadership scale I make the team say what evidence would prove our view wrong, before we act on it.

02 · METRIC INTEGRITY

A metric can recover while the people it measures don't.

The easy fix, putting the timer back, returns acceptance to about 76% within a week, and the weekly report looks solved. It also leaves four people permanently at zero, because nothing in the system ever raises a score on its own.

Proof: replaying the score showed a cliff, not a curve. Twelve people sat at 0.72–1.00 and four at exactly 0.00. Under my fix, 0 of 16 reach the floor.

At leadership scale every headline metric gets a counter-metric for whoever it might be hiding.

03 · PRIORITISATION

Sequence beats speed. The politically easy fix goes last.

Shipping the popular fix first would have let the metric recover around the people still harmed, and taken away the urgency to fix them. I put it sixth of six and explained why in the brief to the director.

Proof: the rescue value came from the replay, not intuition. The system default (0.5) would have put the four below everyone. 0.8 puts them mid-roster.

At leadership scale roadmaps are ordered by what each step makes possible, not by what's loudest.

04 · SYSTEMS OVER HEROICS

Don't just fix the release. Fix how releases get decided.

The release was not a coding mistake. It was a decision that shipped without anyone writing down how we'd know it worked. So I built a review skill that grades every brief against five criteria, scheduled it weekly, and ran it on my own work too.

Proof: first run, four existing briefs, nine flags. Every brief failed on evidence. Not one figure between them. My own brief got one flag, for a step with no owner.

At leadership scale one investigation becomes a standing control the whole org runs on.

05 · AI-NATIVE LEADERSHIP

AI is the instrument. Judgement is the product.

I ran the whole investigation with Claude Code across a wiki, a live database and a codebase. The skill isn't using the tool. It's knowing which question can be answered wrong, and not accepting the first fluent answer.

Proof: four times, an answer that sounded right was overturned by a sharper question, including once when I pushed back in writing on the model's reading of the code, and was right.

At leadership scale I'd build a team that uses AI for speed and is measured on whether its decisions hold up.

Judgement under uncertainty

I changed my mind four times, on the record.

Being right at the end mattered less than being willing to keep testing a conclusion that already felt finished.

What I first believedWhat actually held
Six people were being starved of work.Four. Adjusted for demand, two of the six were up 14–16%.
Distance filters people off the list.Nobody is filtered on distance. I pushed back on the analysis, and the code proved it.
Restoring the timer recovers them.It doesn't. The score floor is a trap. The timer fixes the metric, not the people.
Short lists mean ranking excludes people.It's availability. A different problem, and a different fix.

Every responder's standing, replayed

A hypothesis I killed

I suspected a knock-on effect in a sister product that reads our data. I tested it, it wasn't supported, and it stays in the write-up as a closed question rather than being quietly dropped.

A caveat I put on my own work

The data ends 6 September and it's now November. Step one of my plan is to get current numbers before anyone acts. My own review standard flags stale evidence, and I applied it to myself.

Working with AI

Three questions that changed the outcome.

Force a mechanism
Is the decline in response rate caused by responders ignoring notifications, or by notifications failing to reach them?

Two mutually exclusive answers, not an invitation to tell a story. A question that can be answered wrong is worth asking.

Disagree on the record
I disagree with you that you are excluded from the list when your expected travel time is larger than 45 minutes. [...] What I miss here?

I was right, and the correction went into the permanent record. Treat the model as a colleague who can be wrong.

Own the decision
Maybe Don't count miss as a refusal is most important thing. Then somehow un-stick the four [...] and lastly the decision about the timer.

The recommended plan waited on a colleague we no longer needed. Re-ordering it was a product decision, and I made it.

What I'd bring

My first 90 days in a product leadership role.

The same moves as this case, run across a whole product organisation.

DAYS 1–30

Listen across sources

  • Read the data, the tickets, and the code paths behind the top KPIs
  • Find the users who can't reach us
  • Write down what the org believes and what would disprove it
DAYS 31–60

Make metrics honest

  • Pair each headline KPI with the group it might be hiding
  • Close the instrumentation gaps that make questions unanswerable
  • Re-sequence the roadmap by what each step makes possible
DAYS 61–90

Install the controls

  • A light evidence standard for every brief, enforced automatically
  • AI-assisted research as a team practice, with checks that the answers hold
  • A team that changes its mind in writing
